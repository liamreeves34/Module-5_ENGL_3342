I'm Liam Reeves, a computer science major at the University of Texas Rio Grande Valley, on track to graduate in fall 2027. I've spent the past year as an undergraduate research assistant in machine learning, and I want to build a career in AI: designing models and tools that solve real problems for the people who use them.
When this semester began, I thought effective technical communication in computer science meant being accurate and complete. If the facts were right and every step was there, I assumed the reader would keep up. Writing for real audiences changed my mind. Drafting a Docker instruction manual taught me to start from what the reader already knows and to state plainly what they need before step one. Surveying students and faculty for our parking report showed me that a usability problem only becomes convincing when you can point to the people it fails. Designing a one-page flyer about AI use forced me to cut a page of ideas down to four that a busy classmate would actually read.
Now I think good technical communication in my field works like good engineering. You define the user, test the document against them, and revise until it does its job. Accuracy is where the work starts, not where it ends, and a correct document nobody can follow has still failed. The books on this shelf hold the work that taught me that. Open one to see what I wrote in each module, what challenged me, and which choices paid off.
